WaterBuddy · Creative direction reset · for review

A tidepool you want to visit

The last round organised the product well, but it looked like a well-made form. This round starts again from the experience: a living underwater place, a character worth checking on, and water that behaves like water. Everything below is running live in your browser, not drawn.

Open the working prototype →

The prototype runs the eight moments you asked for: opening, idle, logging 250 ml with real liquid, Buddy reacting, progress changing, a field task, a postcard, and the Waterverse.

01 · Buddy

Three new characters, each tied to a real piece of water biology

I dropped the tardigrade and every droplet idea. Each concept below has a different silhouette, material, personality and way of moving, and each is built in real-time 3D so you can judge it in motion. Every line-up shows the same character in four states, all animating at once. Drag to turn them, or press a button to make them react.

Concept A

Pip, a hermit crab whose shell is a glass bottle

IdleHappySurprisedSleepy
Silhouette

A tilted bottle with a crab in the neck: periscope eye stalks and one big claw, one small. Readable at 24 px.

Material

Clear glass with real liquid inside, a glossy coral shell with a soft sheen, a cork, a paper scroll.

Personality

Shy, curious, a bit dramatic. Hides when startled, peeks back out, shows off with the big claw.

Idle

Each eye looks around on its own. Blinks, antennae sway, the claw clicks, and now and then Pip taps the glass, which ripples the water inside.

Reactions

Ducks into the neck while you pour, pops out cheering. Dances at halfway. At the goal, climbs on top and corks the bottle.

True to life

Land hermit crabs really carry water inside their shells to keep their gills wet.

Risk: real hermit crabs can get trapped in plastic litter. The story must make the bottle a cherished, hand-blown glass home that Pip chose and polishes, never rubbish.

Concept B

Moss, a marimo lake ball that floats when it's full

IdleHappySurprisedSleepy
Silhouette

A perfect fuzzy sphere, two bead eyes and a ring of bubbles. Recognised by its material, not its shape.

Material

Real-time fur from 18 stacked shells, plus glassy bubbles.

Personality

Serene, soft and quietly funny. Never in a hurry.

Idle

Slow roll, breathing bob, fur that sways like it's underwater, sleepy blinks.

Reactions

The fur ripples, bubbles collect as you drink, and Moss floats higher. At the goal it rises to the surface.

True to life

Real marimo rise in daylight on oxygen bubbles and sink at night, on a daily rhythm.

Risk: a ball is the weakest silhouette, the cute-blob space is crowded, and Moss can't naturally carry postcards or travel.

Concept C

Fold, an origami boat-bird folded from a postcard

IdleHappySurprisedSleepy
Silhouette

A paper boat with a crane's neck and folding wings. All sharp angles, unlike every round mascot.

Material

Folded paper with flat-shaded facets and a printed inside.

Personality

A theatrical show-off, slightly vain about its folds.

Idle

Bobs as if afloat, folds its wings like breathing, tilts its head at you.

Reactions

Flaps and hops, spins, tucks its neck to sleep. Later it could unfold flat into the day's postcard.

Why it's interesting

Folding is an ownable motion vocabulary, and it links straight to the journal and postcards.

Risk: almost no face, so emotion is weaker; paper and water is a mild contradiction; good folding animation is expensive to author.

Recommendation

Go with Pip

  • The character is the interface. Pip's home holds today's water, so the most important number on the screen is also the most delightful object on it.
  • Every product moment already has an action. Pour: he ducks and the bottle fills. Goal: he corks it. Night: he sails on it. Morning: a message in the bottle. Postcard: he unrolls it.
  • Expressive without words. Independent eye stalks, a lopsided claw and hiding say a lot. Pip never needs a speech bubble.
  • Right for every age. A cute crab for kids. Introvert humour and real glass-and-liquid craft for teens and adults.

Keep the other two as world material: Moss lives in Loktak Lake as a Waterverse resident, and Fold's origami language becomes how postcards and the journal open and close.

02 · Today

Today becomes a living tidepool

What you see

  • Depth-graded water, brighter near the surface, with drifting light shafts.
  • Animated caustics dancing on the sand, swaying seagrass, drifting motes and a small school of fish.
  • Pip's bottle as the centrepiece, holding today's water. The level is real liquid that sloshes and settles.
  • Gentle parallax as you move your finger or cursor.

What stays readable

  • The number, the goal and the buttons are ordinary page elements over the scene, so screen readers, text scaling and translation all work.
  • A soft paper scrim sits behind the number; at night the text turns light.
  • Logging is always one tap. The animation never blocks the next log, and Undo is right there.
  • If 3D can't run, the same screen works as a flat page.
03 · The Waterverse

A small explorable archipelago, not a map

The Waterverse is a live 3D sea of six real places on the Northeast route, each a handmade island with its landmark: Loktak's floating rings, Umngot's boat floating over clear water, Majuli's river channels, Tsomgo's snowy peak. You drag to look around and tap an island to fly to it.

Progress you can see

Uncharted islands sit under drifting fog and in grey. When Pip's postcard arrives, the fog dissolves, colour floods back, birds start circling, the route inks in rust and Pip's little bottle sails to the new island.

Where it grows

Later chapters sit deeper: coasts and mangroves, reefs and islands, then waters of the world. Each new chapter is a new region of the same sea, so the world gets bigger the longer you stay.

04 · Motion language

Every movement explains something

The rule: motion shows cause and effect in the water. Nothing loops just to look busy, and every moment has a reduced-motion version that keeps the meaning.

05 · Technical approach

Use the simplest tool that reaches the bar

NeedChoiceWhy, and the trade-off
3D renderingthree.js, WebGL 2 todayThe most common web 3D library, with the biggest ecosystem. WebGPU now ships in Chrome and Safari 26, but Firefox on Android still lacks it, so WebGL 2 is the safe baseline. three.js's WebGPU renderer can switch on later and falls back to WebGL 2.
App frameworkReact + React Three FiberThe 3D scene becomes components next to the UI. A thin layer over three.js, so the shaders here carry over unchanged.
LiquidCustom shader, not a fluid simulationA moving cut-off plane, a back-face "cap" for the surface, and wobble from a spring. The standard games technique: cheap, stable and art-directable. A real fluid simulation would cost far more battery for a worse look.
CharacterBlender → glTF (meshopt + KTX2 textures), with procedural animation layered on topAuthored clips for big actions (hide, cork, sail); code-driven eyes, blinks and look-at so Pip reacts to you live. The prototype builds Pip from primitives to prove the motion before modelling.
Water and worldShaders: caustics, toon ocean with depth bands and shoreline foam, noise-based fogNo texture downloads, so it looks crisp at any size. Fog reveal is a single number per island.
2D micro-animationRive for stamps, stickers and buttonsState machines suit interactive UI better than Lottie's playback. CSS covers the rest.
PhysicsSprings and simple particles, no physics engineEverything here is a damped spring or a short-lived particle. Small and predictable.
PWAThe service worker caches the 3D bundle and models after first visitLogging works offline. The page shows the numbers first, then loads 3D, so 3D never delays a log.

Performance budget (proposed)

  • 60 fps on a 2022 mid-range Android phone; drop to device pixel ratio 1 automatically when frames run slow (the prototype already does this).
  • Today scene: under 80 draw calls and 150k triangles; Pip's glTF model under 400 KB.
  • Throttle to 30 fps after 10 s with no input; stop rendering when the app is in the background.
  • No shadows from lights; soft contact shadows are baked into the sand shader.

Accessibility and fallbacks

  • Every control is an ordinary button, never inside the 3D canvas; Waterverse islands are also a list of buttons.
  • Logs, milestones and postcards are announced to screen readers.
  • Reduced motion follows the system setting, with an in-app switch.
  • No WebGL, or a very slow device: the same app as a flat illustrated layout.

Research

What I read

You asked me not to fake research. I couldn't view images or videos, only written sources. These shaped the decisions above:

  • ABZÛ (Giant Squid): interviews describe light shafts and how water takes the colour of the light passing through it, with reds fading with depth. That is why Today grades colour by depth and leans on light shafts.
  • Astro Bot (Team Asobi): a compact frame, a low centre of gravity and expressive, slightly clumsy animation carry the character. Pip is built the same way.
  • Liquid-in-a-bottle shaders: a moving cut-off plane, a back-face cap and wobble from velocity; tilted bottles are the known hard case. That is exactly what Pip's bottle uses.
  • Shell texturing (Lengyel et al. 2001, NVIDIA): the standard real-time fur technique, used for Moss.
  • Toon water tutorials: depth-banded colour and foam where objects meet the water, used for the Waterverse sea.
